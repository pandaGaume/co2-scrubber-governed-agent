# CO2 and people: levels, effects and limits

CO2 is not toxic at the levels of an ordinary room, but it is not inert either. What a level does to people depends on the level and on how long they breathe it.

## Reference levels (at sea-level pressure)

- Outdoor air on Earth: about 420 ppm.
- Occupied indoor rooms: typically 600 to 1,000 ppm; above about 1,000 ppm a room is commonly judged stuffy, and ventilation guidance aims below it.
- Crewed spacecraft: CO2 is held far above Earth's indoor levels, because every kilogram removed costs power and mass. Cabins have commonly run between about 2 and 5 mmHg of CO2 (roughly 2,600 to 6,600 ppm at sea-level pressure).
- Occupational limit (OSHA): 5,000 ppm averaged over an 8-hour day.

## Effects

- From a few thousand ppm, headaches, drowsiness and reduced attention are reported, more often with longer exposure; crews of the International Space Station have reported headaches in the upper part of the cabin range, and NASA has been lowering its crew limits.
- Individual sensitivity varies; effort raises both the CO2 a person produces and their breathing.
- Very high levels (tens of thousands of ppm) cause breathlessness, confusion and loss of consciousness.
